# Trump and the Press: A Chronological Ledger, 2015–2026

*Working document. Compiled October 26, 2026 from the Trump Tracker corpus, the Deep Dives "Shut Out" data on the 2026 White House press ban, and new research for the periods the tracker covers thinly (the 2016 campaign, the first term, the years between the terms and 2025). Every item carries at least two independent sources or a primary document. Every link comes from the tracker's source lists, the Civics Desk and Deep Dives source files, or a page a researcher loaded and saved, and a script checks each one against that record before the file is accepted. Where the record is an allegation rather than a finding, the entry says so.*

## How to read this document

**What counts here.** An act is included when Trump, his White House, his campaign acting for him, or an appointee acting in office took it against the press or against the public's access to government information. Each entry carries the one label that fits it best:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Access and the pool | Rules for who covers the president that apply to everyone: pool selection, seating, ended or cut briefings, removing the press from workspaces. |
| Bans and credentials | A named outlet or reporter shut out, or a credential rule whose effect is exclusion: revoked passes, barred outlets, the Pentagon pledge. |
| Lawsuits and settlements | Suits by Trump, his campaign or his company against outlets, pollsters, authors and publishers; his government's suits to stop books; legal threats in writing; and what was paid to settle. |
| The FCC and licenses | Acts of the FCC and its chairman: license threats and early reviews, investigations of newsrooms, merger reviews and conditions that reach coverage, and pressure on the commission's own members. |
| Public media and Voice of America | The Corporation for Public Broadcasting, NPR and PBS; the U.S. Agency for Global Media, Voice of America, Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty, Radio Free Asia and the Open Technology Fund: defunding, firings, shutdowns, and the rulings on them. |
| Threats and rhetoric | Statements that threaten a consequence (jail, prosecution, a lost license, a lawsuit, a firing) or single out a named reporter or outlet. Generic "fake news" posts are not itemized; published tallies of them are in Appendix B. |
| Leak investigations and subpoenas | Seizures of reporters' records, subpoenas and gag orders, changes to the Justice Department's rules on subpoenaing the press, and leak hunts aimed at the press. |
| Action against named journalists | Official action rather than words: investigations, arrests, detention, deportation, visa actions, border screening, firings at a government-funded outlet, and force used by federal agents on reporters. |
| Information withheld | Public data, records, reports and briefings removed or stopped: website and dataset purges, firing or overruling statistics and science officials over published findings, records rules weakened, reports withheld. |

**Who counts as the actor.** Trump himself, including as a candidate in 2015–16 and 2022–24 and as a private litigant between the terms; his White House and campaign; and his appointees acting in office (the FCC chairman, the attorney general, the FBI director, the secretaries of defense and homeland security, the head of the Agency for Global Media). Republicans in Congress, state and local officials, and the outlets' own decisions are left out unless a government act drove them and a source says so. Appendix A lists what was weighed and left out, with the reason.

**Status language.** "Ruled" names the court and the ground. "Dismissed" says on what ground; a dismissal on standing or for failure to state a claim is not a finding that the reporting was false or true. "Alleged" means the claim rests on reporting that has not been confirmed by a finding, and the entry names the outlets and whether the sourcing is anonymous.

**Ratings.** Items drawn from the Trump Tracker carry its severity rating (catastrophic, severe, serious, moderate, concerning) and a link to the tracker entry, which has the full account and the complete source list; the date shown is the tracker entry's date. Items added by new research, or drawn from the Deep Dives data, carry no rating.

**Overlap with the corruption ledger.** The settlements that media companies paid Trump also appear in `trump-corruption-chronology.md`, as pay-to-play. Here they are counted as acts against the press.

**Years.** Dates are the date of the act where known, otherwise the date it was first reported. A matter that ran on (a suit, a ban and the rulings on it) appears at the date of the act, with the outcome in the same entry.

## The 2016 campaign, June 2015 to January 2017

As a candidate, Trump made the press a target of the campaign itself: his campaign barred outlets by name, a Spanish-language anchor was removed from a press conference, and Trump promised to change the libel laws so he could sue. These are a campaign's acts, not a government's; the government's begin with the inauguration.

## First term, January 2017 to January 2021

In office, the attacks continued in words, from "the enemy of the American People" to calls to challenge network licenses, and moved into the machinery of government. The Justice Department seized reporters' records in leak cases; the White House revoked or suspended press passes until courts ordered them restored and ended the daily briefing; agencies took down data, and NOAA disavowed its own forecasters; and the head of the government's international broadcasters fired the networks' leaders and investigated Voice of America journalists for bias. Courts ruled against the administration in the press-pass cases, in Portland and at the Agency for Global Media.

## Between the terms, January 2021 to January 2025

Out of office, Trump took the press to court. He sued the New York Times, CNN, Bob Woodward, ABC, CBS and the Des Moines Register. The Times and CNN suits were dismissed in 2023 and ABC settled in December 2024; the CBS suit was settled, and the Woodward and Register suits dismissed, after he returned to office. As a candidate he said reporters who would not name their sources should go to jail and that networks covering him critically should lose their licenses, and his campaign barred reporters whose coverage it disliked. As president-elect, in December 2024, he said the suits should have been brought by the Justice Department.

## Second term: 2025

The second term opened with the FCC reviving complaints against three networks and investigating NPR, PBS and a radio station over its reporting, the AP barred over "Gulf of America," the White House taking control of the press pool, and Voice of America's staff sent home. The attorney general rescinded protections against subpoenaing reporters, Congress at Trump's request rescinded the Corporation for Public Broadcasting's funding for 2026 and 2027, and federal officers used force against journalists covering immigration protests in Los Angeles and Chicago; judges issued orders restraining them. In the fall the FCC chairman's warning to ABC's affiliates preceded Jimmy Kimmel's suspension, and nearly every outlet in the Pentagon press corps turned in its badges rather than accept its credential rules.

## Appendix C. Method and sources

**What was read.** The ledger was built on October 26, 2026 from three bodies of material. First, the Trump Tracker corpus: every entry was searched by id, title, `specialTags` and summary for press, media, FCC, public-media, leak, credential and public-information terms, each candidate was read, and an entry was kept only when Trump, his White House or an appointee acting in office was the actor. Sixty-eight entries were kept; those weighed and dropped are in Appendix A. Second, the Deep Dives "Shut Out" data on the September 2026 White House press ban, which supplied two items and the outcome of the ban. Third, new research for the years the tracker covers thinly.

**Tracker items.** Each keeps the entry's date, its severity rating and a link to the entry, which carries the full source list. The one or two sentences after the title were written from the entry's own text, and its three best sources were chosen for document type (court records and government documents first), relevance to the act, and a spread of outlets. Two entries were checked against primary records and corrected here (see below).

**Research.** Five research agents, each assigned a period (2015 to 2020; 2021 to 2024; January to June 2025; July to December 2025; and a narrow 2026 pass for acts the tracker lacked), worked from a written set of rules: two independent sources or a primary document for every item, the outcome of every matter where there was one, verbatim quotations only, and the exact headline of every page cited. Each agent loaded every page it cited, saved its text under `press-chronology/research/<period>/`, and listed it in that folder's `map.tsv`. Every quotation in the researched items was then checked by script against those saved texts. The first-term president's tweets are cited to the American Presidency Project's daily compilations; his Truth Social posts are quoted from outlets that reproduced them, because trumpstruth.org refuses automated access. Paywalled pages were cited only where the headline and opening loaded.

**Merging.** Each act appears once, at the date of the act, with its outcome in the same entry: rulings, settlements and later developments are folded into the entry for the act they concern. Repeated statements of the same threat within days are given as one entry. Where the tracker holds a later development as an entry of its own (the dismissal of a suit, a later ruling), both are kept and cross-referenced. Researched items that duplicated a tracker entry were dropped. Two items carry month-only dates because the record gives no day.

**The rhetoric rule.** Statements are included when they threaten a consequence or single out a named reporter or outlet. The label as first proposed covered named reporters; named outlets were added during the merge so that statements such as "the enemy of the American People," which named five networks, could be included.

**The URL gate.** `press-chronology/gate.py` checks every link in this file against the union of the Trump Tracker's sources, the Civics Desk and Deep Dives source lists, and the agents' `map.tsv` files; it also checks that every tracker link names an entry that exists and that no item title contains an em dash. It exits non-zero on any miss. On the final run it checked 716 links, 586 of them distinct source URLs, and found no misses.

**Tracker discrepancies found.** `bls-commissioner-fired` dated the firing of Erika McEntarfer August 15, 2025; the Bureau of Labor Statistics release and same-day reporting put it on August 1, and this ledger uses that date. `trump-epstein-wsj-defamation-lawsuit-dismissed` presented the defendants' arguments (falsity, malice and damages) as the judge's findings; the order rests on actual malice and special damages and "declines to address" truth and defamatory meaning. `ej-antoni-bls-nomination` was dated August 1, 2025; the nomination was announced August 11 and withdrawn September 30. All three entries were corrected on October 27, 2026, each with a dated correction note. In `media-tech-settlements-86-million-trump-library-ballroom`, one CNN citation carries a headline that differs from the page it links to.

**Limits.** The tracker is dense only from 2026, so the earlier years rest on research that is necessarily selective; the agents' search budgets were capped, and some leads could not be sourced to the standard (Appendix A). Counts of attacks and incidents published by press-freedom groups (Appendix B) include acts by local police and are not added together. Sources documenting single-outlet or anonymous-source claims are named in the entry. The researched URLs were loaded by the agents that cited them, and the tracker URLs come from the tracker's own source lists; the gate proves where each link came from, not that every page will stay up.
