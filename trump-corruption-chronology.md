# Trump Administration Corruption: A Chronological Ledger, 2017–2026

*Working document. Compiled October 25, 2026 from the Trump Tracker corpus (1,271 sourced entries), the Deep Dives corruption data, the Civics Desk corruption explainer, and new research for the periods those sources cover thinly (the first term and the gap between terms). Every item carries at least two sources or a primary document; every URL was loaded and checked before it was written here. Where the record is an allegation rather than a finding, the entry says so.*

## How to read this document

**What counts as corruption here.** An act is included when it fits at least one of these descriptions, and each entry is labeled with the one that fits best:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Self-dealing and emoluments | Public office or public money produced private benefit for Trump, his family or his companies: payments from governments, taxpayer money spent at Trump properties, licensing deals struck in office, government promotion of Trump products. |
| Foreign money | Money from foreign governments or their proxies reaching Trump, his family or their businesses while he held or sought office. |
| Pay-to-play | Donations, memberships, memecoins, dinners, club fees or business deals that bought access, policy or official favors, or were marketed as doing so. |
| Pardons and clemency | Clemency with a donor, business, loyalty or witness-silencing nexus, including clemency that erased restitution owed to victims. |
| Regulatory and prosecutorial favors | Dropped cases, approvals, settlements or waivers that benefited donors, business partners or Trump companies. |
| Appointee misconduct | Cabinet members and senior officials using office for personal benefit: travel, gifts, contracts, undisclosed holdings, or politicking on the public payroll for the president. |
| Trades and disclosures | Securities trading around official actions, and what the president's own financial disclosures show. |
| Public money, private benefit | Taxpayer funds spent on the president's image, properties, projects or allies without the normal process. |
| Dismantling enforcement | Removing the institutions and rules that would catch any of the above: inspectors general, ethics offices, disclosure regimes, enforcement teams. |
| Cover-up | Obstructing oversight or silencing witnesses to any of the above. |

**What is left out, and where it went.** Abuses of power without a money or favor nexus (the January 6 pardons, attacks on the press, immigration enforcement, the Iran war) are tracked elsewhere and are not here. A short list of borderline matters that were considered and set aside, with the reason, closes the document.

**Status language.** "Adjudicated" means a court, inspector general, ethics office or GAO made a finding. "Dismissed on standing" or "mooted" means no court ever reached the merits; it is not an exoneration and the entry does not treat it as one. "Alleged" means the claim rests on reporting that has not been confirmed by a finding, and the entry names the outlets and whether the sourcing is anonymous.

**Ratings.** Items drawn from the Trump Tracker carry its severity rating (catastrophic, severe, serious, moderate, concerning, disputed) and a link to the tracker entry, which has the full account and complete source list. Items added by new research carry no rating.

**Years.** Dates are the date of the act where known, otherwise the date it was first reported. Some matters span years (the emoluments suits, the Trump Foundation case, the pardons' aftermath); they appear at the date of the act, with the outcome noted in the same entry.

## First term, January 2017 to January 2021

Trump entered office without divesting, and his hotels, clubs and licensing business kept operating with him as the ultimate owner. The entries below record who paid, what the government paid, and what the inspectors general, the Office of Special Counsel, GAO and the courts found when they looked. Cabinet and agency-head misconduct appears in the same timeline as the president's own, because the first term's pattern was set from the top.

## Appendix A. Considered and set aside

These matters were weighed and left out of the ledger proper, with the reason. Most are tracked in the Trump Tracker under other categories.

**Abuse of power without a money or favor nexus.** The January 6 pardons and their aftermath; the search of Rep. Ilhan Omar's home; the Justice Department's election litigation; attacks on broadcasters and the press; the Iran war and its costs; the DOGE data seizures; the firing of civil servants. Each is documented in the tracker and in the Rigged Before the Vote and Shut Out projects. They are not corruption as this document defines it.

**The Epstein files.** The tracker holds more than twenty entries on the administration's handling of the Epstein files, filed under corruption because they concern a cover-up. The thing being covered up is not financial, and this ledger keeps to money and favors. One exception is kept in the body: Ghislaine Maxwell's lawyer's statement that she would testify to Trump's innocence if granted clemency, because it describes a pardon offered as a trade.

**Economic outcomes filed under corruption in the tracker.** Deficit and debt milestones, inflation readings, trade-deficit records, tourism and consumer-sentiment declines, gasoline prices, corporate profits and the Spirit Airlines bankruptcy. They are consequences of policy, not acts of corruption, and are not here.

**Regulatory rollbacks without a documented beneficiary nexus.** The FTC's abandonment of the non-compete ban, the Labor Department's abandonment of the overtime rule, the CFPB's gutting, the fall in fraud prosecutions, the EPA's data-center disclosure exemption, the FCC's gigabit goal and router-ban exemption for Netgear, the Interior Department's payments to cancel offshore wind projects (except the $900 million Stonepeak settlement, which is in the body because the firm is run by a Trump donor), the clean-energy grant cancellations, and the farmworker wage cut. Where reporting later ties one of these to a donor or a Trump business, it belongs in the body.

**Branding without public money.** The renaming of the U.S. Institute of Peace, the Kennedy Center and Palm Beach's airport, the $250 bill design, the "Trump Gold Card," and the Trump Store hats handed out at a cabinet meeting. Self-glorification is a tracker category of its own. Items where public funds paid for the president's image (banners, the "paid for by the U.S. government" ads, the gilded statues, the walkway) are in the body.

**Findings that cleared the official.** The Interior inspector general did not substantiate ethics allegations against David Bernhardt (2019 and 2023); the HUD inspector general cleared Ben Carson personally on the dining set (the purchase itself is in the body); the Treasury inspector general found no violation in Steven Mnuchin's flights (in the body, as borderline, because the request for a honeymoon jet was documented); the Postal Service inspector general found Louis DeJoy met his ethics requirements (in the body as borderline). The Commerce inspector general's finding that Wilbur Ross misrepresented the origin of the census citizenship question in testimony is a false-statement finding with no money nexus and is noted here only.

**Alleged conduct that predates office.** The straw-donor scheme alleged at Louis DeJoy's former company (2003–2014) was investigated by the FBI and closed without charges; Tom Price's 2016 pharmaceutical trades as a congressman. Trump's own pre-presidency matters, Trump University and the Trump Foundation, appear in the body because their adjudication came during the first term and the Foundation case found campaign-related self-dealing.

**Single-sourced or unconfirmed.** The text attributed to Ric Grenell offering a donor access to "POTUS" (in the body, marked disputed, because Axios alone has seen it); Tucker Carlson's claim that Trump ordered the Epstein cover-up; the Washington Sun's report that Foreign Service officers were asked how they voted; the Capital One filing that it closed the Trump Organization's accounts for anti-money-laundering reasons (a bank's characterization, not a finding).
